## The one-sentence version

A forensic report answers the questions the client asked, one finding at a time, and every finding
points at the exact piece of evidence that shows it, so someone who wasn't there can check each one.

Think of a maths exam that says "show your working". The answer on its own gets you nothing anyone
can check; the working lets them follow each step and see where it came from.

## Why it matters in security

The report is what the client keeps. Everything before it (the imaging, the hashes, the hours of
reading) matters only as far as the report carries it. A report that can't be checked asks the
reader to trust you; a report where each finding cites evidence lets them see for themselves, and
lets a court, an insurer or a second examiner do the same.

It's also where the client learns what to change. NIST SP 800-61r3 (§3.1) says improvements to an
organisation's defences often come out of the follow-up reports and lessons learned after an
incident. A report that ends without fixes has done half its job.

## How it actually works

### Answer the letter's questions

The letter of authorisation says what may be examined and what the client wants to know. The
report answers those questions, in that order, and stays inside that scope. Something outside it
that you couldn't help noticing gets one line saying it was out of scope, not an examination.

### One finding, one statement, one ref

A <Term id="finding">finding</Term> says one thing: who, what, when (in UTC), where. Under it sits
an <Term id="artefact-ref">artefact ref</Term>, such as `log:security/17` or
`mem:train-lt-02-mem:pid/6120`, naming exactly the record, file or process that shows it. `pin`
collects those as you go, with a note on why each matters, so the report is built from the board and
not from memory.

### Weigh the other explanations

NIST SP 800-86 (§3.4) lists what shapes a report, and the first is alternative explanations: for
each finding, consider every plausible explanation and say which the evidence supports. The
runtime compiler region that looked like injected code, the account sign-in that looked like its
owner: say what you ruled out, and why.

### Know who's reading

§3.4 also names the audience. The client's owner needs plain words and what to do on Monday; a
technical reader needs the refs and the times. The same findings serve both, with a short summary
at the top.

### Evidence decides, whoever it points at

A report says what the evidence shows, even when it isn't what the client hoped. Sometimes that
clears the person everyone suspected. That's the report working.

## See it

The drill's letter asks two questions. Gather the evidence for each onto the board.

    "pin",
    "mem psscan train-lt-02 | grep unlinked",
    "pin",

  task="Type `cat letter.txt` to read the two questions the examination has to answer."

  success="Two pins, two refs: log:security/17 for how the drill got in, and mem:train-lt-02-mem:pid/6120 for what was running that shouldn't have been."
/>

The first pin is `log:security/17`, the remote sign-in: it answers "how did the drill get in, and
when" with a record anyone can open. The second is `mem:train-lt-02-mem:pid/6120`, the unlinked
`svchost.exe`, which answers "what was running that shouldn't have been". Type
`pin -m "remote sign-in from the Range"` instead and the note travels with the ref. Put into words:

1. The account `trainee` signed in by remote desktop (logon type 10) from `192.168.60.66` at
   09:15:10 UTC, after twelve refused sign-ins from the same address. (`log:security/17`)
2. A program named `svchost.exe`, PID 6120, ran from `C:\Users\Public\Downloads`, started by
   `explorer.exe`, missing from the active process list, and checked in with `192.168.60.66:443`
   every minute. (`mem:train-lt-02-mem:pid/6120`)

Then the fixes: block the address, remove `practice-svc`, turn off remote desktop where it isn't
needed, and give `trainee` a password nobody can guess in twelve tries.

## In practice

Every case ends in a report, and every answer has a Supporting evidence picker holding your pins.
An answer counts as supported only when it's right **and** a pin it cites proves it; the debrief
shows each as supported, needs evidence or not yet, and you can change your report as often as you
like. In [Case 1, The Clean Copy](/cases/case-01) that's one pinned note. In
[Case 2, The Deleted Invoice](/cases/case-02) the owner asks you to "confirm it was the
bookkeeper", and the evidence says otherwise. [Case 3, Something Is Still Running](/cases/case-03)
ends with the fixes: what Quillfen Freight should change so the door isn't left open again.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The report is where you explain what you did."** It's where you say what you found. What you
  did goes in the chain of custody, which the report can attach.
- **"A confident answer is enough."** A right answer with nothing cited is one nobody can check. The
  ref is what makes it a finding.
- **"The report should give the client the answer they expect."** It gives the answer the evidence
  supports, and says so plainly when that's a surprise.
- **"Findings are the end."** Fixes are. A client who knows what happened and not what to change is
  still exposed.
