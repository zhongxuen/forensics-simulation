## The one-sentence version

Collect evidence in the <Term id="order-of-volatility">order of volatility</Term>: whatever
disappears fastest comes first, and whatever will still be there next week comes last.

Picture footprints in snow on a sunny morning. The footprints melt first, the snowman lasts until
lunch, and the fence will be there next week. If you photograph the fence first, you lose the
footprints, and the footprints were the part that said who walked through.

## Why it matters in security

The most useful evidence is often the most fragile. Which program is talking to an address outside
the building, which account is signed in right now, what an attacker typed a minute ago: all of
that is <Term id="volatile-data">volatile data</Term>. It lives in memory and nowhere else, and it's
gone the moment the power is.

That's why "turn it off and on again" is the most common way evidence dies. So is the opposite
mistake: spending the first hour carefully copying a disk that wasn't going anywhere, while the
connection you needed quietly closes.

## How it actually works

RFC 3227, a published internet guideline for evidence collection, gives an example order (§2.1),
from most to least volatile:

1. Registers and cache, the processor's own scratch space
2. The routing table, network caches, the list of running programs, and memory
3. Temporary file systems, which are wiped at restart
4. The disk
5. Logs and monitoring data kept on other machines
6. The physical setup and how the network is wired
7. Archive media, such as backups

In practice nobody can capture the processor's registers from outside, so a real collection starts at
item 2. NIST SP 800-86 (§5.2.1.3) goes further inside that item and suggests, first to last: network
connections, who is logged in, the contents of memory, the running programs, open files, network
settings, and the computer's clock.

### Collecting changes things too

On a live machine, every command you run starts a <Term id="process">process</Term> of its own and
takes a little memory. You can't avoid that. What you can do is change as little as possible, and
write down exactly what you ran and when, so the traces you left can be told apart from the ones you
found. RFC 3227 (§2.2) adds a warning worth remembering: don't trust the programs on the machine
you're examining. An attacker may have swapped them. Bring your own tools, on your own media.

### Then the disk

Once the volatile data is safe, the disk gets its turn, as a <Term id="forensic-image">forensic
image</Term>: an exact copy, made without writing to the original. The next two lessons cover
proving who had that copy and proving it's exact.

### It's an order, not a law

The order tells you what's at risk. It doesn't decide for you. If a machine is actively deleting
files, cutting the power may save more than it costs. If the machine is a hospital's booking system,
switching it off has costs of its own. Decide on purpose, write down why, and the decision will
stand up to questions.

## See it

On your workstation, the list of running programs is a good example of volatile data: there's no
file on the disk that holds it.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Switching it off preserves it."** It preserves the disk, and throws away memory, connections
  and the list of running programs. Unplugging the network cable stops an attacker without that
  loss.
- **"The most important evidence goes first."** The most _fragile_ evidence goes first. The disk may
  hold the answer, and it's still collected later, because it will wait.
- **"A good examiner leaves no trace on a live machine."** Every command leaves some. The skill is
  leaving as few as possible and recording each one.
- **"Once memory is captured, the machine doesn't matter."** Memory is one snapshot. The disk, the
  logs on other machines and the backups still hold things memory never did, like what happened
  last week.
