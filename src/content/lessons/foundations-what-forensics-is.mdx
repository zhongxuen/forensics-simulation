## The one-sentence version

<Term id="digital-forensics">Digital forensics</Term> is working out what happened on a computer
from the traces it left behind, in a way that someone else can check step by step.

Think of a fire investigator. They arrive after the flames are out, and they can't rewind the fire.
Everything they conclude has to come from what's still there: scorch marks, a melted plug, a
window left open. Your job on the blue team is the same, with files, logs and memory instead of
ash.

## Why it matters in security

When something goes wrong, people want three things: to stop it, to understand it, and to make
sure it doesn't happen again. Forensics is the understanding part, and the other two lean on it. A
fix aimed at the wrong way in leaves the real door open.

It also matters because your answer will be questioned. The client's insurer, a regulator, a
manager or the person whose account was used may all ask: _how do you know?_ Your conclusions are
only as good as the <Term id="digital-evidence">digital evidence</Term> behind them, and evidence is
only good if you can show it wasn't changed along the way.

## How it actually works

The United States' standards body, NIST, describes forensics in four phases (NIST SP 800-86, §3).
Nearly every team uses some version of them:

1. **Collection.** Find where the evidence lives and copy it, while protecting it from change. Making
   that forensic copy is called <Term id="acquisition">acquisition</Term>.
2. **Examination.** Use tools to pull out what's relevant from the copy: the files, the log records,
   the programs that were running.
3. **Analysis.** Put the pieces together to answer the questions you were asked. When did it start?
   Which account? What changed?
4. **Reporting.** Write down what you found, how you found it, and what the client should fix. Say
   when the evidence supports more than one explanation.

Two ideas run through all four phases.

**Permission and edges.** A case starts with <Term id="authorization">authorization</Term>: a
client who asked, and signed a letter saying so. The letter also sets the <Term id="scope">scope</Term>:
which machines, which accounts, which questions. A personal phone on the desk may hold the answer,
and if the letter doesn't cover it, it stays out.

**Nothing you do should change the evidence.** Every phase after collection works on the copy, and
you write down each step so that another examiner could repeat it and get the same result. That
repeatability is what turns "I think" into "here is how you can check".

## See it

Your practice workstation has a small examination laid out on it. Start the way every case
starts: read the letter.

Notice what the letter gives you: _what_ you may examine (one stick), _what for_ (two questions),
and _who_ said so. The copies folder is the collection phase already done. Reading the note is
examination. Deciding when it was written, and whether you trust that, is analysis.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Forensics is about catching someone."** It's about finding out what happened. Sometimes the
  evidence clears the person everyone suspected, and that's a result worth as much as any other.
- **"Forensics only happens when the police are involved."** Most investigations are for the
  organisation itself: to understand a break-in, fix it and tell the right people. The same care
  makes the report hold up either way.
- **"The expert looks at the machine and knows."** Conclusions come from steps someone else can
  repeat. If you can't show how you got there, it's an opinion.
- **"Collect everything."** The scope decides what you may collect, and the questions decide what's
  worth it. A copy of every device in the building is slower to examine, and some of it you had no
  right to take.
