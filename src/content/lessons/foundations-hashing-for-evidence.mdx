## The one-sentence version

Work out a <Term id="hash">hash</Term> of the original when you collect it, work out one of your
copy, and if the two match, the copy is exact down to the last bit.

Imagine checking two copies of a thousand-page book by reading them aloud side by side. A hash does
that comparison in a moment, and it doesn't get tired: one changed comma on page 612 still gives a
completely different result.

## Why it matters in security

Every conclusion in your report rests on the copy you examined. If anyone can argue the copy differs
from the original, every conclusion can be argued with too. A matching hash ends that argument: it
shows the <Term id="integrity">integrity</Term> of the copy, meaning nothing about it changed.

It also protects you from yourself. Hash your copy at the start of the work and again at the end,
and you can show your own examination changed nothing, which is harder to promise than it sounds.

## How it actually works

A hash recipe reads every bit of its input and produces a short, fixed-length fingerprint. Change
anything, even one bit, and the fingerprint changes completely. The same input always gives the same
fingerprint, on any computer.

### When to hash

NIST SP 800-86 (§4.2.2) describes the routine. Connect the original through a write-blocker, so the
examiner's computer can't write to it. Work out the original's hash. Make the

<Term id="forensic-image">forensic image</Term>. Work out the image's hash. If they match, the image
is exact. Record both, with the time.

Timing matters. A hash proves nothing changed _since it was taken_. If the original was altered
before anyone hashed it, the hash will happily vouch for the altered version. That's why NIST IR
8387 (§3.2) says to hash as close to collection as possible, and to store the hashes somewhere the
examiner can't quietly change them.

### Which recipe

<Term id="sha-256">SHA-256</Term>, from the United States' Secure Hash Standard (FIPS 180-4, §6.2),
gives a 256-bit fingerprint, written as 64 characters. It's the one to rely on today.

<Term id="md5">MD5</Term> (RFC 1321) gives a 128-bit fingerprint, written as 32 characters. It was
the forensic standard for years, and you'll still see it on old handover forms. The problem is
<Term id="collision">collisions</Term>: two different inputs with the same fingerprint. For MD5,
people can now manufacture them on purpose, in about a minute on a laptop (RFC 6151, §2.1).

What that means for evidence is subtle. MD5 still catches accidental change: a bad cable or a
careless examiner won't happen to produce a collision. What it can't rule out is deliberate forgery,
where someone prepares two different versions of a file that share one MD5. So examiners record
SHA-256, and often MD5 as well, to match older records. Never MD5 alone.

### What a hash doesn't tell you

A hash says two things are identical. It doesn't say where the original came from, who had it, or
whether the contents are true. Those questions belong to the chain of custody, which is why the two
always travel together.

Your workstation has two copies of a note from a practice USB stick, a handover form with the hash
taken before handover, and a file of hashes worked out for each copy. Which copy is exact?

all 64. The two hashes here were worked out from those very files, so they're genuine. Try
`cat copies/copy-b/note.txt` to find the changed character yourself.

## In practice

carries a SHA-256 taken on site. You image the laptop, then check your image's hash against the
form. If you look at the original before imaging it, with the write-blocker off, reading it updates

reset and do it again.

## Common misconceptions

- **"A matching hash means the evidence is genuine."** It means the copy matches the original at the
  moment the hash was taken. Whether the original is what someone says it is is a custody question.
- **"MD5 is broken, so MD5 hashes are worthless."** MD5 still catches accidental change. It can't
  rule out a deliberate collision, which is why you record SHA-256 too.
- **"Looking doesn't change anything."** Opening a disk without a write-blocker can update
  last-opened times, and a single changed time changes the whole hash.
- **"The hash is stored with the image, so it's safe."** If one person can change the image and the
  hash together, the hash proves nothing. Keep the record somewhere separate.
