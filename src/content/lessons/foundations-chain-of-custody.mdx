## The one-sentence version

A <Term id="chain-of-custody">chain of custody</Term> is the record of everyone who had the
evidence, what each of them did with it, and when, from the moment it was collected to the moment
the report is signed.

Think of a parcel's tracking history: collected, sorted, loaded, delivered, each with a time and a
place. If it says "delivered" and there's no "loaded", you'd want to know where the parcel spent
that time. Evidence is the same, and the question will be asked more sharply.

## Why it matters in security

Your report says "the note on this laptop was created on Saturday evening". Someone who doesn't like
that answer won't argue with the timestamp. They'll ask who had the laptop between Saturday and the
day you copied it, and whether anyone could have changed it. A chain of custody is how you answer.

NIST SP 800-86 (§3.1.2) spells out what the chain involves: a log of every person who had the
evidence, what they did to it and at what time, storing it securely when nobody is using it,
examining only a copy, and checking that the original and the copy still match.

## How it actually works

A chain of custody isn't one document. It's several habits that together leave no gaps.

**A custody log.** The <Term id="custody-log">custody log</Term> is the running list: date and time,
who, and what they did. "Took the laptop from the yard office." "Handed it to Idris, seal intact."
"Opened the bag, made a copy." Each entry is short. The rule is that every change of hands and every
action on the evidence gets one.

**Seals and labels.** Evidence goes into a bag with a numbered seal. A log entry that says "seal
0412 intact" is something anyone can check later, and a broken seal with no entry is a question.

**Acquisition that doesn't change the original.** <Term id="acquisition">Acquisition</Term> is
making the forensic copy. It's done with a <Term id="write-blocker">write-blocker</Term> between
the original and the examiner's computer, so nothing can be written to it by accident, and the log
says it was on.

**Hashes, kept apart from the evidence.** A fingerprint of the copy, worked out as soon as it's
made, lets anyone check later that it hasn't changed. NIST IR 8387 (§3.2) adds the detail that makes
this work: store the hashes somewhere separate from the image, where the person examining it can't
quietly change both. The next lesson is about those fingerprints.

**Work on the copy.** The original goes back in a sealed bag. Every examination happens on the copy,
and if the copy is ever in doubt, you make another from the original and check it the same way.

### What a gap means

A gap doesn't mean someone tampered with the evidence. It means you can't show they didn't. The
honest thing is to say so in the report, explain what you know, and let the reader weigh it. Hiding
a gap is far worse than having one.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Chain of custody is paperwork for court."** Most cases never reach a court. The same record is
  what lets a client, an insurer or a colleague trust your conclusion without redoing your work.
- **"A good hash replaces the custody log."** A hash proves two things are identical. It can't say
  where the original came from, who had it, or whether it was changed before anyone hashed it. Both
  are needed.
- **"Only the original needs a chain of custody."** Copies need one too. If you can't say which copy
  you examined and where it came from, the chain breaks at the copy.
- **"If there's a gap, the evidence is worthless."** A gap weakens it and has to be explained. It
  doesn't erase it. Say what happened, plainly, and let the reader decide.
